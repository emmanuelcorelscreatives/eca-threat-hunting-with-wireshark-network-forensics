## Intake, prediction, and coverage

Before examining packets, the authored mapping puts fictional WKS-17 at `192.0.2.10` from `2025-01-16T11:55:00Z` until `12:10:00Z` (exclusive). The note expects a workstation-initiated TCP/80 HTTP exchange with `updates.office.example` (`198.51.100.20`) during **12:00–12:05 UTC**. Predict that packets supporting that *documented expectation* will match its time, peer, direction, and protocol. A second destination at the same time might be other legitimate software or an additional, undocumented maintenance peer; neither alternative is established by timing alone.

`capture-info.stdout.txt` reports **22 packets**, Ethernet classic PCAP, microsecond timestamp precision, earliest packet `2025-01-16 12:01:00.000000`, latest `12:02:15.160000`, and duration **75.160000 seconds**. `all-frames.stdout.txt` explicitly gives `frame.time_utc` for frames **1** and **22** as `Jan 16, 2025 12:01:00.000000000 UTC` and `Jan 16, 2025 12:02:15.160000000 UTC`. The recording covers only part of the five-minute note window. It provides no independently measured clock offset, collector placement, process inventory, or visibility beyond the file. The capinfos `File size` is **1,883 bytes**; its `Data size` is **1,507 captured frame-data bytes**, not HTTP file content.

The executed `tcp-conversations.stdout.txt` has `Filter:<No Filter>` and two TCP rows. `192.0.2.10:54001 ↔ 198.51.100.20:80` starts **5.000000000 seconds relative to the first capture packet**, lasts **0.1600 seconds**, and totals **9 frames/582 frame bytes** (left-to-right **6/380**, reverse **3/202**). `192.0.2.10:54002 ↔ 203.0.113.77:80` starts **75.000000000 seconds relative to that first packet**, lasts **0.1600 seconds**, and totals **9 frames/575 frame bytes** (left-to-right **6/373**, reverse **3/202**). ## Three checked evidence cards

All frame citations here refer to **the packaged `practice.pcap`** and the named fields in `all-frames.stdout.txt`; reproduce them in your own unchanged working copy before citing that copy. `frame.time_utc` is a human-readable UTC rendering, not an ISO-8601 field. `tcp.seq_raw`/`tcp.ack_raw` below are **absolute values from the export**, not Wireshark’s possibly relative sequence-number display.

1. **A — candidate timing and endpoints:** Frame **14**, `Jan 16, 2025 12:02:15.000000000 UTC`: `ip.src=192.0.2.10`, `tcp.srcport=54002` → `ip.dst=203.0.113.77`, `tcp.dstport=80`; `frame.protocols=eth:ethertype:ip:tcp`, `tcp.stream=1`, `tcp.seq_raw=2000`, `tcp.ack_raw=0`, `tcp.len=0`, `frame.len=frame.cap_len=54`. Frames **15–16** show reverse and forward zero-payload TCP packets, including frame 15’s `tcp.ack_raw=2001` and frame 16’s `tcp.ack_raw=8001`. The JSON specification assigns the three packets S/SA/A flags, but **TCP flags were not exported in this executed TShark fields log**: do not claim a logged flags field. This supports locating an outbound connection sequence within coverage, not its application purpose. An authorized application could have initiated it; packets alone cannot identify the process or approval.

2. **B — candidate visible exchange, not merely a handshake:** Frame **17**, `Jan 16, 2025 12:02:15.060000000 UTC`, `192.0.2.10:54002 → 203.0.113.77:80`, `tcp.stream=1`: `frame.protocols=eth:ethertype:ip:tcp:http`, `http.request.method=GET`, `http.request.uri=/status`, `http.host=other.office.test`, `tcp.seq_raw=2001`, `tcp.ack_raw=8001`, `tcp.len=49`, `frame.len=frame.cap_len=103`. Frame **18**, `12:02:15.080000000 UTC`, reverses the endpoints and reports `http.response.code=200`, `http.file_data=4f4b` (hex for the **two application-body bytes** `OK`), `tcp.len=40`, `frame.len=frame.cap_len=94`. Thus the fixture shows an HTTP request and a response, **not** a 94-byte downloaded file or proof of a software update. `/status`, the client-supplied Host header, and `200` could fit several legitimate or unrelated activities; they do not identify the process, actual service owner, or intent. Frame **13** separately reports `dns.qry.name=other.office.test` and `dns.a=203.0.113.77` at `12:02:10.020000000 UTC`; this decoded DNS answer is not ownership verification.

3. **C — packet comparison with the documented peer:** Frame **6**, `Jan 16, 2025 12:01:05.060000000 UTC`, `192.0.2.10:54001 → 198.51.100.20:80`, `tcp.stream=0`: `frame.protocols=eth:ethertype:ip:tcp:http`, `http.request.method=GET`, `http.request.uri=/manifest`, `http.host=updates.office.example`, `tcp.seq_raw=1001`, `tcp.ack_raw=5001`, `tcp.len=56`, `frame.len=frame.cap_len=110`. Frame **7**, `12:01:05.080000000 UTC`, reverses the endpoints, with `http.response.code=200`, `http.file_data=4f4b` (**two body bytes**), `tcp.len=40`, `frame.len=frame.cap_len=94`. Frame **2** at `12:01:00.020000000 UTC` reports `dns.qry.name=updates.office.example`, `dns.a=198.51.100.20`. These packets match the note’s **listed** peer, time, outbound direction, and HTTP protocol; they do not show that maintenance software made the request or that a manifest/update was actually obtained. Compare their peer and Host with frames 17–18: the candidate uses **`203.0.113.77` and `other.office.test`**, not the listed destination. A different legitimate process—or an unlisted maintenance peer—remains possible. DNS and HTTP labels cannot settle ownership.

The log’s `tcp.len` is TCP payload length, which can include HTTP headers and body; `http.file_data=4f4b` denotes only two visible response-body bytes. Conversation `Bytes` and `frame.len` count recorded frames, while capinfos `File size` includes capture-file overhead. The supplied field export does not independently validate checksums. `malformed.stdout.txt` is empty for the executed `_ws.malformed` filter, meaning **no frames were reported by that check**, not that every possible defect or missing packet was ruled out.

## Four-way test and stop condition

- **Time:** Both HTTP sessions occur within the authored 12:00–12:05 UTC note window and the *observed* 12:01:00–12:02:15.160 UTC capture span (`frame.time_utc` for frames 6, 17, 1, 22). Neither their proximity nor the apparently precise synthetic times verifies a real collector clock or process schedule.
- **Peer:** Frame 6 uses the note’s one listed `198.51.100.20` destination; frame 17 uses `203.0.113.77`. The note **does not assert that its list is exhaustive**. A mismatch with the documented peer warrants follow-up; it cannot establish hostility or exclude all scheduled-software explanations.
- **Direction:** The requests in frames 6 and 17 are workstation-to-peer; frames 7 and 18 show replies. This fits the note’s initiated-direction claim for the listed peer but does not distinguish what software initiated the candidate.
- **Visible protocol/exchange:** Both include TCP/80 HTTP GETs and HTTP `200` replies (`http.request.method`, `http.response.code`), but `/manifest` and `/status` and their Hosts differ (`http.request.uri`, `http.host`). Matching HTTP alone does not prove common purpose. The `OK` bodies are not application or process attribution.

**Result for the synthetic candidate:** The narrow assertion that it went to the note’s **one documented destination** is contradicted by frames 14/17. Its attribution to **scheduled software as a whole remains inconclusive**: the destination list is not certified exhaustive, process-to-connection records are absent, and clock/path coverage is not independently established. The documented-peer exchange is consistent with the note, not proof that its named software ran. Neither the schedule comparison nor unfamiliarity of the other peer establishes compromise. Ask for authorized endpoint process/network records covering the window and activity-owner confirmation of all expected destinations. If a reliable, exhaustive list later excludes the candidate, scheduled-activity attribution could become **unsupported** without making the session malicious; if reliable observations and context truly match, it could be **supported**. Do not record either requested answer as packet evidence.
